## Detail

### 1. Domain ownership & expiry — 🟢 Green

**Observed.** `robertos.com` was registered on **1996-04-27** and expires **2027-04-28** — 217 days
after the audit date. Registrar is GoDaddy.com, LLC. The registry lists transfer/update/delete
locks (`clientTransferProhibited` et al.), the normal protective status codes. Registrant identity
is privacy-redacted (standard since GDPR), so *who controls the GoDaddy account* is not verifiable
externally.

**Why a buyer should care.** A domain registered in 1996 is a genuinely scarce asset — a short,
brandable .com held for 30 years — and it anchors the restaurant's reservations, ordering, and
search presence. Expiry is comfortably out, so there's no renewal fire drill. The open question is
control, not existence.

**Ask the seller.** Who holds the GoDaddy account credentials (owner personally? a web designer?
an ex-employee?), and will the domain be transferred to a buyer-controlled registrar account at
close? The transfer lock must be lifted by whoever controls that account — make that a closing
checklist item.

### 2. DNS & hosting fingerprint — 🟢 Green

**Observed.** Nameservers are `ns8.wixdns.net` / `ns9.wixdns.net` (Wix). The domain resolves to
three Wix A records (185.230.63.107/.171/.186). MX records point at Google (detail in checks 5–6).

**Why a buyer should care.** DNS is delegated entirely to Wix, meaning the website *and* the DNS
zone live inside one Wix account. That's operationally simple — one account to transfer — but it
also means that single account is a single point of failure for web, DNS, and (indirectly) email
routing.

**Ask the seller.** Confirm the Wix account email/owner, and that the same account controls the
DNS zone. Any change of ownership must transfer this account, not just "the website."

### 3. SSL/TLS certificate — 🟢 Green

**Observed.** Valid certificate for `robertos.com`, issued by Google Trust Services, valid
2026-08-13 → **2026-11-11** (50 days remaining at audit time). Short-lived certs like this are
typical of platform-managed (auto-renewing) TLS.

**Why a buyer should care.** The cert is valid and consistent with Wix managing it automatically —
no action needed, and renewal should continue without human involvement as long as the Wix
subscription stays paid.

**Ask the seller.** Nothing beyond check 2's account-transfer question — the cert follows the Wix
account.

### 4. Website platform (CMS) fingerprint — 🟡 Yellow

**Observed.** The site self-identifies as **Wix** (generator meta tag: "Wix.com Website Builder").
CloseCheck flags every detected platform yellow by design: it reports what it observed and does not
assert patch/vulnerability status from a fingerprint alone — that's a manual confirmation item.

**Why a buyer should care.** Wix is hosted SaaS, so there's no unpatched server to inherit — good.
But the site, its content, its connected apps (reservations/ordering widgets), and its subscription
billing all live in a Wix account that must change hands. Wix sites can be transferred between
accounts; it's a defined process, not automatic at closing.

**Ask the seller.** Which Wix plan and which connected apps (OpenTable/ordering/booking widgets)
the site uses, who pays the subscription, and a commitment to execute Wix's site-transfer flow to a
buyer-owned account at close.

### 5. Email authentication posture — 🟡 Yellow

**Observed.** SPF record present (`v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all`) — correct for the Google
Workspace setup found in check 6. **No DMARC record exists.** No DKIM record was found at common
selectors — which is *not* evidence DKIM is absent (Google typically uses a custom selector);
CloseCheck reports this as note-only, never as a scored finding.

**Why a buyer should care.** SPF-without-DMARC is the classic half-finished email setup: anyone can
send mail claiming to be `@robertos.com` and receiving servers have no published policy telling
them to reject it. For a restaurant this is a live fraud vector (fake "gift card" or "reservation
deposit" emails to customers, fake invoices to suppliers) and a deliverability drag — and it's the
buyer's problem the day after close. It's also a ~30-minute, zero-dollar fix.

**Ask the seller.** Ask them (or their IT contact) to publish a DMARC record — even `p=none` with a
reporting address is a strict improvement — and to confirm DKIM signing is enabled in the Google
Workspace admin console. Both are reasonable pre-close asks.

### 6. Email provider fingerprint — 🟢 Green

**Observed.** MX records point at `aspmx.l.google.com` and the four standard Google alternates —
**Google Workspace**.

**Why a buyer should care.** Business email runs on a mainstream, transferable provider — no
obscure host or ISP mailbox to migrate off. The Workspace admin account joins the list of accounts
that must be handed over.

**Ask the seller.** How many Workspace users/mailboxes exist, who is super-admin, and whether any
mailbox (e.g., the owner's personal address) is entangled with accounts the business depends on
(POS, suppliers, the Wix and GoDaddy accounts from checks 1–2).

### 7. HTTPS hygiene — 🟢 Green

**Observed.** `http://robertos.com` redirects to `https://robertos.com/`, and the site sends an
HSTS header (`max-age=31556952`), instructing browsers to use HTTPS only for a year.

**Why a buyer should care.** Visitors can't land on an unencrypted page, and the HSTS policy is a
small but real sign the platform defaults are sane. Nothing to fix, nothing to inherit.

**Ask the seller.** Nothing — this one is clean.

## What Tier A can't see — and what Tiers B and C add

Everything above was observable from the outside, with zero seller cooperation. That's the point of
Tier A: it's the free, fast pass that finds the "who actually controls this?" questions *before*
you're in a data room. What it deliberately cannot tell you:

- **Tier B (document-verified).** Does the seller *own* the domain and the Wix account, or does a
  web designer from 2015? Is there a contract behind the online-ordering commissions? Who owns the
  Google Business Profile that drives the bookings? Tier B items are verified against documents the
  seller provides — registrar account screenshots, platform invoices, vendor agreements — and each
  finding cites its evidence.
- **Tier C (seller-attested).** The things no document fully proves — "no other admin accounts
  exist," "the POS data will convey," "no side agreements with delivery platforms" — captured as
  structured, signed seller attestations, so the buyer has recourse language, not verbal assurance.
