# Why QoE Reports Miss Technology Risk — and How Diligence Consultants Are Adding It as a Service Line

A quality of earnings engagement answers one question with rigor: are the earnings real? Revenue recognition, add-backs, customer concentration, working capital — the financial machinery of the business, verified.

But on a main-street acquisition — a restaurant, a salon, a franchise unit, a small e-commerce brand — a growing share of those verified earnings flows through technology assets that the QoE scope never touches. The online-ordering account producing a third of weekly sales. The booking platform that holds the entire client list. The domain name, which it turns out is registered to a web designer nobody has heard from since 2019. The POS contract that auto-renews for three years with a termination penalty, sixty days after your client closes.

The earnings were real. The *systems that produce them* were never checked — and in the sub-$2M market, if the QoE provider doesn't check them, usually nobody does. There's no CTO on either side of the deal, no IT diligence firm in the budget, and the buyer assumes "due diligence" covered it.

## The gap, concretely

Technology diligence on a main-street business isn't an engineering assessment. There's no codebase. It's a **transfer-of-assets problem** in three layers, and each layer maps to work QoE professionals already know how to do:

**1. Public-record verification (no seller cooperation needed).** Who legally owns the domain and when does it expire? Is the website's platform current or abandoned? Is the Google Business Profile claimed, and by whom? Does email authentication exist? These checks run from public data — registration records, DNS, certificates — and can be completed before an engagement letter is even signed. They regularly surface the deal-relevant findings: a domain the seller doesn't control, a listing administered by an ex-employee, a site running software years out of date.

**2. Contract analysis (seller-provided documents).** The POS agreement's renewal and termination terms. The transferability clause — or its absence — in the online-ordering and booking platform agreements. Payment processor statements: holds, reserves, chargebacks. The full recurring-software inventory with a transfers-or-rebuilds determination per line. This is document diligence, the skill QoE teams exercise daily, applied to a category of contract the financial workpapers skip.

**3. Live-access verification (near closing).** Credentials actually work. Data actually exports. Account recovery contacts actually change to the buyer's. Attested item by item, the way closing checklists already work.

Nothing on that list requires a software engineer. All of it requires the discipline QoE providers already sell: structured evidence collection, severity judgment, and a report the buy side can act on.

## Why this is a service line and not a favor

Consultants who spot these issues today usually handle them ad hoc — a paragraph in the QoE report, an email flagging the domain thing, an "you'll want to check on the DoorDash account" on a call. Ad hoc has two problems: it's unpriced, and it's uneven. The deals where nobody happens to notice are the deals where the buyer eats the finding after closing — with the diligence provider's name on the report that didn't mention it.

Productizing the same work changes both:

- **Scope becomes explicit.** A defined checklist, tiered by what access it requires, means the engagement letter can say precisely what was and wasn't covered — which is also what protects you when a finding falls outside it.
- **The deliverable becomes billable.** A standalone technology assessment section — or a separate report — is a line item, not a favor. Buy-side clients on main-street deals routinely pay four figures for focused diligence workstreams; a technology assessment priced as a fraction of the QoE fee is an easy conversation when the alternative is "nobody checks this."
- **Repeatability compounds.** The second restaurant deal reuses the first's checklist. The findings language sharpens. The assessment stops depending on which team member happens to know what DMARC is.
- **The external layer is a business-development tool.** Because the public-record checks need no seller cooperation, they can run pre-LOI — on a deal a prospect is merely considering. A two-page external scan on a live deal is a concrete, useful artifact to put in front of a potential client, which is more than most diligence marketing can say.

## What it takes to add it

The honest version: less than it looks like, if you structure it.

- **A checklist that fits the market.** Enterprise tech-DD frameworks (built for software targets with engineering orgs) don't map to main-street businesses. The checklist has to be built around domains, listings, POS and platform contracts, and account transferability — per business type, because a restaurant's risks (delivery platforms, menu sync) aren't a salon's (booking data export, membership liabilities) aren't a franchise unit's (franchisor-mandated stack, system account transfer).
- **A way to run the public checks consistently.** Registration lookups, DNS, certificates, platform fingerprinting — individually trivial, collectively tedious, and worth automating so the junior person isn't hand-assembling screenshots.
- **A findings format the buyer can read.** Traffic-light severity with a note per red item travels better than prose. It also aggregates cleanly into the executive summary the deal team will actually read.
- **A report that carries your brand.** The assessment is your work product, delivered under your firm's name, alongside or inside the QoE deliverable.

Some firms build this internally — a shared checklist document, a junior analyst running lookups, a report template. That works, at the cost of the partner time spent making it consistent.
